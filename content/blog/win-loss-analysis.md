## Win-loss analysis: how to find out why you really lose deals

Win-loss analysis is a simple habit: after a deal closes, won or lost, you find out from the buyer why they decided the way they did, then change one thing in how you sell. The lost reason your rep picks in the CRM is a starting point, not the answer. **Buyers often give a different reason when someone asks them directly**, and many lost deals never went to a competitor at all. They ended in no decision.

## What is win-loss analysis?

Win-loss analysis is a regular review of closed deals that compares what your team thinks happened with what the buyer says happened. HubSpot describes a win-loss review as an interview that works out why an opportunity was won or lost, run either by your own team or by a third party ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/questions-to-ask-win-loss-review)).

It covers three outcomes, not two:

- **Won.** Why they picked you, so you can do it again on purpose.
- **Lost to a competitor.** What the other option did better, or seemed to do better.
- **No decision.** The buyer stayed with what they had, or the project quietly stopped.

Wins deserve as much attention as losses, because they show which habits actually close deals.

## Why is the lost reason in your CRM often wrong?

Because it is the rep's best guess, written at the moment they least want to think about the deal.

Clozd, a company that runs win-loss interviews for B2B firms, tells the story of a proposal team that lost a deal worth nearly a million dollars. In the review meeting, each person gave a confident reason, from thin engineering answers to weak references. When one team member finally called the buyer, the real reason was that the price looked too low to be believable ([Clozd](https://www.clozd.com/blog/why-did-we-lose)). Every internal explanation had been wrong.

Price is the reason that hides the most. Clozd co-founder Spencer Dent says "we lost on price" is not precise enough to act on ([Clozd](https://www.clozd.com/blog/interview-the-influence-of-pricing-on-deal-outcomes)). From his interviews with buyers, the usual causes behind it are:

- **Value not justified.** The buyer could not defend the amount inside their own company.
- **Wrong package.** The offer was bigger than they needed, so a smaller, cheaper option looked safer.
- **Risky pricing model.** They could not predict what it would cost next year.
- **Lost trust in the number.** A high first quote followed by a deep discount made the whole price feel arbitrary.

HubSpot gives the same warning: treat a vague "price" answer as a cue to keep asking gently, because price problems usually trace back to how value was explained ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/questions-to-ask-win-loss-review)).

## Why does "no decision" need its own lost reason?

Because it is a different problem from losing to a competitor, and it is common. Research behind the book _The JOLT Effect_ analysed 2.5 million recorded sales conversations and found that 40 to 60% of lost deals ended in no decision ([Challenger](https://challengerinc.com/losing-to-customer-indecision/)).

The same research found that when buyers got cold feet near the end, 73% of reps went back to arguing that the buyer's current situation was a problem. In 84% of those cases, that approach made a loss more likely ([Challenger](https://challengerinc.com/losing-to-customer-indecision/)). Buyers stuck at the end were usually afraid of making the wrong choice, not unconvinced that they needed one.

If "no decision" gets filed under price or competitor, you will fix the wrong thing. Give it its own reason, and look at what those buyers needed to feel safe. We look at how to handle stalled deals stage by stage in [sales pipeline stages](https://heigin.com/blog/sales-pipeline-stages).

## How do you run win-loss analysis in a small sales team?

Keep it light: a reason on the day a deal closes, a short call with the buyer within a few weeks, and a monthly look at the patterns. You do not need a research team or a special tool to start.

![Five-step win-loss routine for a small sales team. Step one, on the day the deal closes, the rep picks one reason from a short list and adds a line in their own words. Step two, choose which deals to review: every large loss, a sample of wins and every no decision that reached the quote stage. Step three, within a month, someone other than the rep calls the buyer for 15 to 20 minutes. Step four, once a month, group the answers into themes and compare them with the logged reasons. Step five, change one thing in how you sell and check the next month's deals](/images/blog/win-loss-analysis-routine.webp)

1. **Log a reason on the day it closes.** Use a short list: value or price, timing, product fit, competitor (name it), no decision, and other with a note. Add one sentence in the rep's own words.
2. **Choose which deals to review.** Every large loss, a sample of wins, and every no decision that got as far as a quote.
3. **Call the buyer within a month.** In Clozd's 2025 State of Win-Loss survey, companies were more than twice as likely to be happy with the depth of feedback when it was collected within the first month after a deal closed ([Clozd](https://www.clozd.com/state-of-win-loss)). HubSpot suggests keeping the conversation to 15 to 20 minutes ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/questions-to-ask-win-loss-review)).
4. **Have someone other than the rep ask.** The owner, a manager or a colleague from another team. Buyers are often polite to the person they dealt with. In the same Clozd survey, companies using an outside interviewer were far more likely to be satisfied with the depth of feedback than those running it in-house, 70% against 34%, though Clozd sells that service ([Clozd](https://www.clozd.com/state-of-win-loss)).
5. **Review once a month and change one thing.** Group the answers into themes, compare them with the reasons reps logged, and pick a single change to try. Then watch the next month's deals.

## What questions should you ask in a win-loss interview?

Ask open questions about the decision, not about the rep. You want the buyer to tell the story of how they chose. This short list is adapted from HubSpot's longer set ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/questions-to-ask-win-loss-review)):

- What was the biggest factor in your decision?
- What criteria did you use, and who else was involved?
- How did our quote, and the time it took to arrive, compare with the others?
- Why did you decide now, or why did you decide not to change anything?
- What was the biggest difference between us and the other options?
- What is one thing we should do differently next time?

If the answer is price, follow up with: "Too high compared with what: the other quote, your budget, or what you expected to get for it?" Then listen. Do not defend the quote or try to reopen the deal on the call.

## Does win-loss analysis actually improve win rates?

The evidence mostly comes from surveys by firms in the sales business, but it points the same way. In Clozd's 2025 survey, 63% of companies said win-loss analysis had raised their win rate, rising to 84% for programmes that had run for more than two years ([Clozd](https://www.clozd.com/state-of-win-loss)).

HubSpot cites a 2017 CSO Insights study which found that teams who regularly used win-loss analysis saw win rates rise by 14.2% and quota attainment by 17.6% ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/questions-to-ask-win-loss-review)). Read both as direction rather than a promise. The gain comes from acting on what buyers say, not from asking alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between win-loss analysis and a lost-deal review?

A lost-deal review usually looks at one deal, often from the sales team's point of view. Win-loss analysis looks at many closed deals, both won and lost, and adds the buyer's own explanation. The patterns across deals are what make it useful.

### Who should interview the buyer after a lost deal?

Ideally someone who was not in the deal, such as the owner, a sales manager or a colleague from another team. Buyers tend to soften feedback for the rep they dealt with. Some companies hire an outside firm to run the interviews.

### How soon after a deal closes should you ask for feedback?

Within a month, while the buyer still remembers the details. Clozd's 2025 survey found companies were more than twice as likely to be happy with the depth of feedback when it was collected in the first month. Log the rep's reason on the day the deal closes.

### What are the most common closed-lost reasons?

Most teams use a short list: price or value, timing, product fit, a named competitor and no decision. "No decision" deserves its own entry, because research behind The JOLT Effect found that 40 to 60% of lost deals ended that way. Always add a free-text note so the reason can be checked later.

### Is price really the main reason deals are lost?

Reps cite it often, but it is rarely the full story. A Clozd co-founder says "we lost on price" usually hides something else, such as value that was not justified, a package that was too big or a pricing model that felt risky. Ask a follow-up question before you cut prices.
